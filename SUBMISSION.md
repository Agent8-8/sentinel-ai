# Sentinel AI: fraud screening for small online stores

**Track:** AI + Finance  
This project is a prototype and is not financial advice. Payments and verification are simulated.

## The problem

A small online store can lose money to fraudulent orders, but rejecting legitimate customers can lose sales too. Sentinel explores how a store might balance these costs when reviewing card transactions.

## What we built and added

The Finance brief suggests card fraud detection. Sentinel adds a simulated small-store setting: it uses prior order behavior to estimate fraud risk, then compares assumed costs of allowing, verifying, or declining an order. The dashboard replays orders and explains signals behind a decision. An anomaly detector can send unusual orders for verification. It does not connect to a payment service.

## How it works

We generated simulated merchant histories with delayed or missing chargeback reports. A gradient-boosted model uses transaction, velocity, and device-linkage features from earlier orders. We compared it with simple rules and transaction-only models. Separate training, validation, and later test periods help check the model on orders it did not train on. Calibration and threshold selection use the simulator's complete validation labels, including fraud that would be unreported in practice; a real merchant would not have that information.

## Results and failures

In a corrected replay of one simulated store's 21,596 test orders, the full policy incurred **$14,775.53 in simulated costs**, compared with **$77,219.07** for approving everything. The replay schedules chargebacks only for orders that incurred a simulated fraud loss. These are outcomes under assumed costs and customer behavior, not measured savings at a real business. Earlier three-store policy results assumed chargebacks would arrive even for blocked orders; they have not been recomputed with the correction.

The original three-store ranking analysis caught 91.2% of simulated fraud versus 41.0% for the rules baseline, with each threshold set retrospectively to flag 0.5% of genuine test orders. That uses test labels and is a ranking comparison, not a deployable cutoff.

For a separate score-only check on one simulated store, a threshold chosen using validation data produced **93.8% precision and 88.7% recall** on the later test period: 377 frauds flagged, 25 genuine orders flagged, and 48 frauds missed. One missed order was simulated friendly fraud, which the simulator intentionally makes resemble legitimate orders. These figures do not describe the full policy.

In that same exploratory check, 4 of 217 genuine guest orders were flagged, compared with 21 of 20,954 genuine non-guest orders. This is a small simulated group and a possible friction concern, not a demographic fairness conclusion.

We also reran a chronological test on the public ULB/Worldline credit-card dataset: average precision was **0.791** for the boosted model and **0.754** for logistic regression, with overlapping bootstrap intervals. The dataset has no account or device identities, so this does not validate the behavioral signals, verification, or simulated savings.

## Next steps and sources

Test on independent merchant data with realistic delayed labels, examine customer friction across groups, and measure verification outcomes before any real payment use. The simulation cannot establish real-world safety or fairness.
