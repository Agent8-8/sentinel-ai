Sentinel AI: autonomous fraud prevention for small online stores

Sentinel scores each card-not-present order and decides by itself whether to allow it, send the customer to automated verification, or decline it. All results below come from data the model never saw during training or tuning, averaged over three independently simulated stores unless marked otherwise.

How it was tested

Setup

Public card datasets either strip out customer identity or can't test new attack types and late labels. I built a simulator of a 91-day US online store: about 85,000 orders from 16,000 customers, with real account, card, device, IP and shipping-address structure. It includes hard genuine cases (travellers, VPN users, new phones, gifts, shared family devices, a holiday rush) and adaptive fraud (card-testing bots, account takeover through local proxies, stolen cards on fresh accounts, friendly fraud). A "sleeper ring" fraud pattern appears only in the test window.

Chargebacks arrive 5 to 60 days late, and some fraud is never reported. At training time only 47% of training-window fraud was labelled, which is the position a real merchant is in.

Timeline (simulated days)

Train · days 0–56→Gap · 56–63 (labels maturing)→Validation · 63–74 (all model choices)→Test · 74–91 (scored once)

Leakage controls are covered by automated tests. Rewriting every future transaction leaves all past features bit-identical. "Known fraud" signals only use chargebacks that had already arrived. Training labels are cut off at day 63. The test window was scored once, after the configuration was frozen.

Finding 1

Behavioural features are where the accuracy comes from

Finding 2

Pricing each decision in dollars cuts cost by a third over fixed thresholds

For each order Sentinel estimates the expected cost of allowing it (fraud loss plus chargeback fee), declining it (lost margin plus damage to a good customer relationship) and verifying it (friction, abandonment, fraud that passes verification), then takes the cheapest option. A $9 order and a $900 order with the same risk are treated differently.

Finding 3

Supervised models miss new attack types, and a verification-only safety net fixes most of it

To test new attacks, I removed one fraud pattern entirely from training and measured how much of it the model caught. A purely supervised model was nearly blind. An anomaly detector trained only on genuine traffic, allowed to send the most unusual 0.5% of orders to verification (never to decline), caught most of the missing pattern.

Live system

The live replay matches the offline evaluation

The whole test window of one store (21,596 orders, $2.5M volume) was replayed order by order through the running API, the same feature engine, model and policy used offline. Live cost was $14,776 against $14,490 offline; the difference comes from live-only instant feedback and different verification outcomes. In the replay:

82%fraud dollars stopped ($54.5k of $66.6k)
0.024%genuine customers declined
0.55%genuine customers asked to verify
14 msscoring time per order

Each decision carries reason codes computed by switching off one signal group at a time and measuring the drop in risk. Gemini can turn these into plain-English explanations and a daily briefing. It never makes the decision, and without an API key the system uses template text. The Gemini path has not been tested with a real key.

Limits

What these results don't show

  • The main benchmark is synthetic. The fraud patterns are my model of reality. Absolute numbers won't transfer to a real store; the comparisons between approaches are the claim.
  • The sleeper ring is not a true zero-day. It shares device and address fingerprints with fraud crews seen in training. The held-out-pattern results (61–80% with the safety net) are the more realistic estimate for new attacks.
  • On real data the gain is modest. Boosting beat logistic regression with overlapping confidence intervals, and ChatGPT's XGBoost scored slightly higher on its split.
  • Costs are assumptions. Margin, fees, verification pass rates and abandonment all vary by merchant; the What-if view exists for this reason.
  • Verification is simulated. Real 3-D Secure or one-time-code pass rates, especially when a fraudster controls the victim's phone, vary widely.
  • Single-merchant scope. Production systems also use consortium data, device fingerprinting and card-issuer intelligence, which a small store's model doesn't have.
